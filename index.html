01

What the map shows

Each dot is a grid node with published demand (offtake) connection headroom — the load a new connection could draw there today, as stated by the operator's own capacity publication. Generation (injection) capacity is a different market and only ever appears as the separately-labelled grid-strength proxy layer.

Coverage is assembled market by market — regulator → licensed-operator registry → each operator's capacity publication — then normalised into one schema.

02

Headroom is shared

Nearby nodes draw on the same upstream circuits and transformers. Published figures are non-additive: a large load at one node consumes its neighbours' margin too.

Never sum two dots into one bigger site.

03

The firm-power engine

The headline tiles come from a chronological battery simulation on a full year of measured system load per market (15/30-minute resolution). A candidate load T draws continuously; a battery of duration D (energy = T×D, ~88% round-trip) discharges through every interval in which load + T would exceed the year's observed peak — and may recharge only when spare room exists under that peak, through the same connection. Back-to-back constraint days with thin recharge windows are exactly where a candidate load fails.

Uptime = share of intervals at full T; any shortfall counts the whole interval as downtime (the SLA view). 99.9% ≈ 8.8 h of curtailment a year. The slider moves required uptime; tiles recompute from precomputed simulations.

The capability ceiling is the observed single-year peak — a deliberate floor (allowing just +3% roughly triples the 2 h figure). System-level screening: local constraints still gate any specific node.

04

Flexible load, no battery

The "flexible load" tile follows the Nicholas Institute's Rethinking Load Growth method (Norris et al., 2025): the maximum constant load addable against the observed peak such that the energy it sheds stays ≤1% of its annual energy. Note the metric difference — that is an energy tolerance for a load that itself flexes; the battery tiles use uptime, which is stricter.

05

The fit score — Neura funnel

Each demand node is scored 0–100 for the firming wedge at your chosen target load: 45% firming-gap economics (the sweet spot where published headroom covers most-but-not-all of the target, so a right-sized battery bridges the rest), 20% voltage class, 15% industrial land within 3 km, 20% queued-demand pressure within 30 km where the market publishes a queue.

The score ranks screening effort — it is not a bankability rating.

06

Published vs measured

Published headroom is an operator statement, not a measurement. Where substation-level metered load exists (currently Portugal's E-Redes; Great Britain at ~4,000 nodes pending API keys), we compute the real load-duration curve and the empirically firmable added load at 2 h / 8 h battery — nodes marked measured with an orange ring on the map.

The two can disagree in both directions. Where a published figure exceeds measured minimum headroom, treat the published number as the optimistic bound.

10

What none of this is

Screening, not a connection study. System-level firm figures are not nodal promises; nodal headroom is not additive; a fit score is not a bankability opinion; single-weather-year results move with the weather. Every deal this surfaces still ends in a formal access request and site-level verification — this map exists to make sure you spend that effort in the right places.
